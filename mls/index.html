The guide

Five steps. The last one is the whole point.

Steps one through four get you a search that works. Step five is what turns it from a party trick into something that shows up whether or not you remember it.

01

Install the browser extension

Claude has an extension that lets it see and act inside the tab you already have open. Install it in the browser you actually use for work, not a second one you will forget about. Give it a minute after install and check that the sidebar opens on a normal page before you point it at anything that matters.

02

Sign in to your MLS the normal way

Same login, same two factor, same everything. This is the part agents get wrong when they imagine what this is. Nothing is scraped from the outside and nothing bypasses a paywall. It works inside the session you are already entitled to, with exactly the permissions your subscription gives you, which is also why the rules section below matters.

03

Describe the pull out loud instead of clicking filters

Open the sidebar on your MLS search page and say what you want the way you would say it to a new assistant. Towns, date range, price band, property type, status, and what you want back in the list. Be boring and specific. The clearer the sentence, the less you have to fix on the other end. Examples are in the next section.

04

Check the first run against a search you already trust

Run one pull by hand that you know the answer to, then run the same one this way and compare. Count the rows. Spot check three addresses. If the numbers are off, the fix is almost always in your sentence, not in the tool. Do not schedule anything you have not verified at least twice by hand.

05

Turn the prompt that worked into a scheduled task

Once a prompt returns exactly what you wanted, save it as a recurring task instead of retyping it. Pick a time before you start calling, keep the wording identical to the version that worked, and have it report the same fields in the same order every day so you can read it at a glance. This is the step that changes your mornings. Everything before it is setup.

Write the schedule around your day, not around the market. If you call expireds at nine, the list should be waiting at eight, not landing at eleven when you are already in the car.

Example prompts

Say it like a person. Not like a database.

These are written the way you would actually say them. Swap in your towns, your price bands, your property types. The left column is what you say. The right column is what you should get back, and what to check before you trust it.

"Pull every single family in 01701, 01702 and 01760 that expired or was withdrawn between January first and today. Give me the address, the original list price, the last list price, days on market, and the listing agent and brokerage."
Your expired list. Check that the date range caught the boundary days on both ends, and that withdrawn and expired are both in there if that is what you asked for. The price spread between original and last is your whole conversation.
"Show me everything in Framingham, Natick and Ashland that took a price reduction in the last seven days. Sort by the size of the cut, biggest first, and show me the percentage as well as the dollars."
Motivation, ranked. Run this one on the same weekday every week so the seven day window never overlaps or leaves a hole. The top of this list is the shortest path to a price conversation.
"Find every two to four unit multifamily that came on the market in the last thirty days under nine hundred thousand, anywhere in Metro West. Include the unit mix and any rents listed in the remarks."
Inventory for your investor list. Rents in remarks are unreliable by nature, so treat them as a starting point. Confirm the unit count against the listing itself before you send anything to a client.
"What came on the market in Wayland and Sudbury since Friday over one and a half million? Tell me which ones are new construction and which ones had a prior listing in the last two years."
Your farm, every Monday. The prior listing history is the useful half. A house that failed to sell last year and just came back on is a different conversation than a fresh one.
"Show me sales in Marlborough over the last two years where the owner mailing address on record is different from the property address, and give me the sale date and price."
Absentee owner style search. Only works if your MLS carries that field, and it varies. If it does not, this becomes a public records job rather than an MLS one.
"Build me the listing appointment numbers for 4 bedroom colonials in Sudbury: last twelve months of solds, average days on market, list to sale ratio, and current months of supply."
Prep, current instead of stale. Ask it to show the underlying count for every average. A ratio built on six sales and a ratio built on sixty are not the same number, and you will get asked.

One rule on how you phrase these. Keep every search about the property and the transaction, never about who lives there or who you imagine the buyer is. Fair housing applies to how you search and prospect exactly as much as it applies to how you advertise, and a sentence you would not say to a fair housing attorney is a sentence you should not type here either.

Read this part twice

This is your license. Not a hobby.

I am not going to hand you an automation idea and skip the part where it can cost you. Nothing here is legal advice, and your MLS rules beat anything on this page. Check them first.

  • Read your MLS rules before you automate anything. Every MLS has its own policy on access and use, and they are not the same from one market to the next
  • Respect any terms about automated access. Some are explicit about scripted or automated retrieval, some are silent, none of them like finding out after the fact
  • Check your board and your brokerage too. Your broker has an opinion about this and you would rather hear it now than in a compliance email
  • Start with pulls you are already allowed to run by hand. If you cannot legitimately run the search yourself, scheduling it does not make it allowed
  • Do not redistribute what you pull. Display and distribution rules apply to the output exactly as they apply to the screen it came from
  • Never share your MLS credentials. This runs in your own signed in session, and that is the only way it should ever run
  • Watch the first several scheduled runs. Sites change, layouts change, and a silent wrong answer is worse than an obvious failure
  • Nothing goes out without you. A pull is research. The moment it becomes outreach, Do Not Call, consent, and opt out rules are yours to follow

If any of that reads as excessive, keep in mind that the downside here is not a bad week. It is your access, your board standing, and the thing your entire income runs through. Slow is fine. Suspended is not.

After you have the list

Getting the list is the easy half. The list is not the business.

Do this for two weeks and you will hit the real problem. You will have a good list every morning and no more hours in the day than you had before. That is where most agents quietly stop, and it is where the actual work starts.

01

Know who is worth calling

Thirty expireds is not thirty conversations. Something has to rank them before you dial, using history, price behavior and what the record already tells you about the owner. Otherwise you call in the order the list happened to print.

02

Have something to say to each one

The same script across a whole list is why the list stops working by week three. Every name deserves a first line that could only be about that property, and writing thirty of those by hand is not a thing you will keep doing.

03

Follow up past the second attempt

Almost nothing on a morning list converts on day one. The money is in weeks two through nine, which is exactly the stretch that gets dropped when a closing goes sideways and the list keeps arriving anyway.

Each of those is a system, not a prompt. They are what I have spent the last year building on my own business, and they are the parts I do not put in a free guide, because they took a long time to get wrong enough to trust.
