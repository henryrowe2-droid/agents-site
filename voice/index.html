The voice profile, free

Your clients can tell. They just do not tell you.

AI writing has a smell. People catch it in the first line, and what you are spending when you send it is not your time, it is your credibility. This is the whole method for building a voice profile, so the things you write with AI read the way you actually write. It takes about twenty minutes, once. Nothing here is held back and nothing is behind an email form.

They can tell.

Not because the writing is bad. Because it is smoother than you have ever been, and they have been reading you for months.

Nobody replies to say your email sounded like a machine. They just answer a little slower, and a little shorter, and you never find out why.

The part everyone recognizes

Seven tells. You already know all of them.

You have felt these without naming them. Here they are named. Any one of them on its own is forgivable, plenty of humans write this way on a bad day. Four of them in a single message is a fingerprint, and it is not yours.

"Great news on the appraisal, and the timeline still works, so we are in good shape."
The long dash habit. The one that is not on your keyboard and takes a key combination most agents have never bothered to learn. Look at your own sent folder. You use commas and periods, because you are typing between showings. When a long dash shows up three times in a short email, that is the machine signing its work.
"I hope this email finds you well."
Nobody has ever said this out loud. It is the opening of a letter from someone who has never met you. Read your last five sent texts. You do not warm up, you start in the middle, usually with their first name and then the actual point.
"Let us delve into what this means for your search."
Words you do not own. Delve, navigate, landscape, robust, seamless, elevate, leverage. Nobody standing in a kitchen at an open house has ever used the word delve. The vocabulary is a size too big for the room.
"In today's market, buyers need to be prepared."
It says nothing, at length. Every market is today's market. This is the phrase a model reaches for when it has no local knowledge, which is exactly the thing you were supposed to be providing. A real agent says what happened on Cedar Street last Tuesday.
"The bright, airy, and inviting kitchen opens to the living space."
Three adjectives, zero facts. Someone who has stood in that kitchen tells you the sun holds on the sink until about two. The stack of three is what gets written by something that has never been in the room, and every agent reading it knows that.
"So excited to connect! Looking forward to hearing from you!"
Exclamation points doing emotional labor. Warmth lives in what you noticed and remembered, not in punctuation. When the enthusiasm is being carried entirely by the mark at the end of the sentence, it reads as performed, and performed reads as insincere.
"One clean thought. Then a second clean thought. Does Thursday work for you?"
The rhythm no human has. This is the loudest tell and the hardest to see. Real writing runs long, then stops short. It has a fragment. It has an aside you would cut if you cared more. Three even sentences and a tidy question, paragraph after paragraph, is a metronome, and people hear it even when they cannot name it.
Why prompting harder does not fix it

You asked for the average. The average is the smell.

The instinct is to fix tone with adjectives. Professional but friendly. Warm, not pushy. Confident and approachable. It feels like the right instruction, and it is the reason your output still sounds like everyone else's.

The model has read an enormous number of professional but friendly emails. When you ask for that, you get the exact center of all of them. The center is nobody. It is the midpoint of every agent who has ever written a follow up, and that midpoint is precisely what reads as fake to the person receiving it.

Adjectives describe writing, they do not reproduce itTwo agents can both be accurately called warm and direct and write nothing alike. The label is true about both and useful for neither.
Everyone is typing the same instructionThe agent in the next town is asking for professional but friendly too. Your neighborhood is receiving both, and they rhyme.
The distinctive parts are the parts you cannot nameYou do not know that you always open with a first name and no greeting, or that you never use a semicolon, or that you close with a question about half the time.
Correct is the opposite of what you wantAsk a model to write well and it will smooth out the run on sentence, the fragment, and the strange word you always use. Those three things were you.
The method, in full

Stop describing your voice. Go get evidence of it.

The move is simple and almost nobody does it: give the model your actual writing and make it work out what is distinctive about you, then keep that answer forever instead of retyping adjectives every time you open a blank box. Six steps. Do them in order.

01

Gather things you already wrote

A handful of real emails, a few texts, and a listing description you were proud of. Ten to twenty pieces is plenty. Do not write anything new for this exercise. Anything composed on purpose is you performing yourself, and it will contaminate the whole thing.

02

Ask what is distinctive, not what is good

Paste them in and ask how this person writes differently from a typical real estate agent. Sentence length and how much it varies, greetings and sign offs, punctuation habits, contractions, words used constantly, words never used, how they open, how they ask for something, how they deliver bad news. If you ask what is good, it will hand you a list of improvements, and improving is exactly how you lose the voice.

03

Push back once, then once more

The first pass is flattering and vague. Tell it to be more specific and more critical. What are the habits I would not admit to, what do I overuse, what would somebody who knows me well notice in the first sentence. The second pass is where it stops being a compliment and starts being a description.

04

Have it written down as a profile

Ask for the whole thing as one reusable block of instructions, specific enough that a model which never saw your samples could still follow it, with short examples pulled from your own writing. Save it somewhere you can reach in three seconds. This block is the artifact. Everything before it was just process.

05

Use the profile instead of explaining yourself again

From now on the profile goes on top and the request goes underneath. Write the follow up. Write the listing description. Write the note after the showing. You never type professional but friendly again, and because it is the same profile every time, the voice stops drifting from message to message.

06

Test it blind before you trust it

Have it rewrite something you actually sent, without looking at your original first. If you cannot tell which one is yours inside a line or two, the profile is working. If you can tell instantly, you just found the tell that is missing, so add it and run the test again.

What counts as a good sample

The messages you did not think about are the best ones you have.

This is the step people get wrong, and it is the one that decides whether any of it works. Polish is the enemy here. You are trying to capture how you write when you are not trying to write.

  • Fast beats polished. The email you fired off between showings is more you than the one you rewrote three times
  • Sent beats drafted. A draft is what you meant to say, a sent message is what you actually say
  • Texts beat essays. Short messages carry more of your rhythm per word than anything else you produce
  • Ordinary beats impressive. Do not curate your best work, curate your most typical Tuesday
  • Range beats volume. Good news, awkward news, and a direct ask. Fifteen mixed samples beat fifty of one kind
  • Yours beats your team's. Anything a coordinator or a marketing template touched is somebody else's voice wearing your name

One housekeeping note that matters. Take the names, addresses, and phone numbers out before you paste anything into any tool. Client information stays client information, and a voice profile does not need a single real detail to work.

Where this stops

It captures how you write. It does not know what you know.

I would rather tell you the limits now than have you find them at the worst moment. A voice profile is a narrow tool that does one job extremely well, and here is where the edge of it is.

01

Voice, not knowledge

The profile knows your rhythm. It does not know the seller's timeline, what is sitting in inventory this month, or what came back on the inspection. All of that still comes from you.

02

It will not save a bad message

If the thinking underneath is wrong, sounding like you makes it worse. Now it is a bad message in your voice, with your name at the bottom, and it is more convincing.

03

You still read every word

Every line, before it goes anywhere. This shortens the distance between a blank page and something worth sending. It does not take you out of the sending.

What the profile is actually for

This is the smallest piece. It is also the one everything else needs.

Most people build a profile, use it for emails, and stop there. That is a fine outcome and worth the twenty minutes on its own. But the reason I care about this particular step is that it is a prerequisite. Once your voice exists as a saved thing rather than something you re-explain from memory every morning, it can be handed to work that runs whether or not you are sitting there.

Then

The same voice on messages you did not personally write.

The follow up that goes out in month four. The note after a showing. The listing description at eleven at night. Same profile, same person, whether you drafted it at your desk or approved it from a parking lot with one thumb.

Then

Drafts that are already written and waiting on you.

Once the voice question is settled, it stops being how do I say this and becomes who am I saying it to this morning. That is a different kind of system and it is the one that actually changes the shape of your week. I am not going to explain how it is built on a free page.

Always

Nothing reaches a client without you reading it.

However far any of this goes, the last step stays human. Fair housing, Do Not Call, and a clean opt out are not features you bolt on at the end, and nothing I build sends on its own. That rule is older than the tools and it outlives them.

If you do nothing else here, do the profile. You will be further along than you think, and the next thing gets much easier to build.
