Seven systems run my week. You should start with one.

This is the whole stack, written out. Seven things I actually run in my own business, what each one does, why it matters, what it took to get right, and where each one falls down. Then the part almost nobody does, which is picking one and building it properly instead of five of them badly. The whole guide is on this page. No email, no form, no sequence chasing you on day three.

Before the seven

The tools are not the problem. The order is.

Almost every agent I talk to has already tried this. They pay for something, use it hard for nine days, and then quietly stop. The tool was fine. What was missing was a decision about which single part of the job it was supposed to carry, and a way to tell whether it was carrying it.

So before the list, four things that go wrong, all of which I did myself first.

You bought access, not a systemA subscription is a blank box. It will happily do anything once and nothing on a schedule. The value is in the thing that runs whether or not you remember to open it.
You built five things halfwayFive half built systems produce output you do not trust, and output you do not trust is worse than none, because now you are checking everything twice.
You automated a step you never actually didIf you were not calling expireds by hand, a list of them appearing every morning changes nothing. It just makes the not calling more visible.
You never wrote down how you workYou cannot hand off a process you have never described. Most of what people call an AI problem is really an undocumented process problem wearing a new hat.

The numbers on this page

Four figures, and the arithmetic under each one.

I am going to use the same four numbers throughout this guide, so here they are with their assumptions attached. They come from one audit of one real database, mine, run in full.

1,314

370
No contact of any kind in the past ninety days
909
No record in the file of ever having been called
$222,000
An estimate, at the assumptions spelled out below

The last one is arithmetic, not a forecast, and it is worth being precise about how it was built. Take the 370 contacts who have gone dark. Assume 2 percent of them transact with you after you reconnect. That is 7.4 transactions. Multiply by a $30,000 average commission and you get $222,000.

Every one of those inputs is an assumption you can argue with, and you should. The reactivation rate is a number I picked because it felt conservative for people who already raised their hand once, and your average commission is your own. Your result could be far lower. It could be zero. I am not promising you a dollar. The point of the calculation is that leaving 370 people untouched is a decision with a price tag, and most agents have never once put a number on it.

The stack, all seven

Here is everything I run. Nothing held back except how.

Each one gets the same four things: what it does, why it matters, what it took to get right, and the honest caveat. I am describing outcomes, not architecture. How these are built is the part I sell, and I would rather tell you that plainly than pretend this page is the whole thing.

01

The day's prospecting list is ranked and waiting before I wake up.

I do not open a laptop and decide who to call. The list is already built, already ordered, and already sitting on my phone when I get up. It has run every morning since April.

Why it matters. The expensive part of prospecting was never the dialing. It was the forty minutes of deciding, which is where the day quietly goes. Removing the decision is what makes the calling actually happen, because there is nothing left between you and the first number.

What it took to get right. Three rewrites of what ranked means. My first version sorted by how recent something was, which put the freshest name on top every day and buried people who were far more likely to talk to me. Recency and intent are not the same thing, and it took weeks of calling the list to feel the difference.

The honest caveat. It produces a list. It does not produce a conversation. If you are not going to call, this is an expensive way to feel organized. And the list gets scrubbed against Do Not Call before it reaches me, every time, without exception.

02

It reads the call, builds the plan, writes the messages, and then stops.

After a conversation, the follow up is drafted and sequenced and waiting for me. Every message, in my voice, with the timing already decided. Then it holds. Nothing goes anywhere until I have read it.

Why it matters. Follow up is not hard, it is just relentless, and it is the first thing to go in a busy week. Deals do not die from bad conversations. They die in the gap between a good one and the next contact, and that gap is where almost all lost business lives.

What it took to get right. The voice. Early drafts were competent and completely unlike me, and a client who has read you for months can feel that instantly. It took building a real voice profile out of things I had already sent before the output stopped reading like a stranger writing on my behalf.

The honest caveat. The approval step only works if you actually read. Approving a queue with your thumb while walking to the car is not a safeguard, it is a liability with extra steps. If you would not have sent it yourself, do not send it.

03

An audit that tells me who I have quietly abandoned.

It reads the database and surfaces three things: who has gone dark, who has never been called at all, and the promises I made in my own notes that were never delivered. That last one is the one that stings.

Why it matters. On my own file it found 370 people dark past ninety days and 909 with no record of a call ever. Those are not cold leads bought from somewhere. Those are people who raised a hand and got no answer. It is the cheapest business in your entire pipeline and it is already paid for.

What it took to get right. Reading notes properly. Structured fields are easy. The valuable material is a half sentence someone typed eighteen months ago that says will circle back in the spring about the rental, and finding those reliably was harder than anything else in this system.

The honest caveat. It is only as good as what you wrote down, and it is deeply unflattering. Mine listed things I had said I would do and did not. Run it anyway. Being uncomfortable for one afternoon is much cheaper than the alternative.

04

The listing appointment prepares itself the moment it is booked.

An appointment goes on the calendar and the research is assembled without me asking. Property history, the comparable picture, what the neighborhood has done recently, the likely objections, and a point of view on pricing strategy going in.

Why it matters. Preparation is the whole difference between an agent who is present and an agent who is prepared, and sellers can tell inside four minutes. It also removes the worst version of this job, which is doing serious research at eleven at night for a nine in the morning appointment.

What it took to get right. Teaching it to say when it does not know. The first versions filled every gap smoothly, which is exactly the failure mode you cannot tolerate walking into a stranger's kitchen. Now it flags thin data instead of papering over it, and that single change made the output trustworthy.

The honest caveat. Research is not a relationship, and public record is wrong more often than people think. Verify anything you are going to say out loud. The preparation gets you to the door ready. It does not win the listing for you.

05

One brief on my phone before seven, and I know the shape of the day.

Calendar, calls due, new leads overnight, open loops nobody has closed, what the ads spent, and one number about my market. Short enough to read standing up.

Why it matters. Most agents start the day inside their inbox, which means the day is set by whoever emailed last. Ten minutes of knowing what is actually in front of you changes what you do with the first hour, and the first hour tends to decide the rest.

What it took to get right. Cutting it down. My first brief was a page and a half and I stopped reading it by the second week. It got useful when I made it painful to add anything new, and the current rule is that a line has to earn its place or it comes out.

The honest caveat. A brief you glance at is a habit, not a system. If a week goes by and nothing you did changed because of it, that is real information, and the right response is to delete it rather than keep decorating it.

06

The week I actually had becomes the week of content I post.

What happened, the showing that went sideways, the inspection nobody expected, the number that moved, comes back as a week of posts written the way I talk.

Why it matters. Content dies from the blank page, not from a lack of ideas. You lived through five usable things last week and told nobody. Nothing in this is invented, which is the only reason it sounds like a person and the only reason I am willing to put my name on it.

What it took to get right. A voice profile first, built out of my own writing rather than out of adjectives. Before that everything came back smooth, tidy, and faintly like every other agent in the state. That step is free and it is written up in full on the voice page.

The honest caveat. It can only work with what actually happened. A quiet week makes quiet posts, and no system will save you from that. Also, anything touching housing gets the same fair housing read as a listing description, because a post is advertising too.

07

Every past client hears from me on their closing date, with something real.

The anniversary of the closing comes around and there is a note ready to go, with an actual update about their home rather than a greeting card sentiment. It is the smallest system here and the one clients mention most.

Why it matters. Past clients are the only part of the business that compounds, and they go stale in silence. The date is already sitting in your file. Almost nobody uses it, which is exactly why it lands when you do.

What it took to get right. Making it worth opening. A generic happy anniversary is noise and people can smell the automation on it. It became something clients actually reply to when it carried a real number about their own house and one specific memory from the transaction.

The honest caveat. Be careful how you frame value. An estimate is an estimate and should be labeled as one, because a homeowner will quote you on it later. And if the anniversary note is the only time they hear from you all year, they will know that too.

The rule under all seven

Every one of them stops and waits for you.

Not one of these systems talks to a client on its own. They draft, prepare, rank, research and remind, and then they hold. The last step is a person, and on my systems that person is me. Your name is on the sign and on the license, and neither of those transfers to software.

The part that actually decides this

Build one thing. Then use it for a month.

Here is the plainest thing in this guide. Most agents fail at this by building five systems badly instead of one system they use. It is not a discipline problem and it is not a technical problem. Five half finished things produce output nobody trusts, and the day you stop trusting the output is the day the whole project ends, usually without anyone announcing it.

So do it in this order. It is slower on paper and much faster in practice.

01

Pick the thing you already do every single day

Not the thing you wish you did. The thing that is genuinely already in your day, that you would notice missing tomorrow. If you do not currently follow up after calls, follow up is the wrong place to start, because automating a habit you do not have gives you a beautifully organized version of nothing.

02

Write down every step of it by hand

All of it, in order, including the parts that feel too obvious to write. Where you look first. What makes you skip someone. What you check before you hit send. This document is the actual work. Anyone who has tried to build one of these will tell you the same thing: the building was easy and the describing was hard.

03

Run it manually for a week, exactly as written

Follow your own steps by hand for five business days. You will find three things you left out and one step that turns out to be pointless. Better to find those now, on paper, than to discover them baked into something you have already been trusting for a month.

04

Only automate the parts you are genuinely using

By now you know which steps you skip and which ones carry the weight. Automate the ones you use. Leave the rest alone. A small system that runs every day beats a complete one that impresses people in a screenshot and gets opened twice.

05

Use it for thirty days before you build anything else

This is the step everybody skips and it is the whole difference. Thirty days tells you whether it survives a closing week, a bad Tuesday, and a vacation. If it is still running after that, you have a system. Then, and only then, pick the second one.

If you did only the first system on this list and nothing else, you would be ahead of most of the agents you compete with. That is not motivation. It is just what the field currently looks like.

Not optional, ever

The rules are not the boring part. They are the part that keeps your license.

Speed is exactly what makes this dangerous. A person sends one bad message. A system sends four hundred of them before lunch, in your name, with your license number at the bottom. Everything below is what separates an agent who is still doing this in ten years from an agent explaining a complaint to their broker.

  • Fair housing in ad targeting. Housing ads live under rules other advertising does not. Audiences built on protected characteristics, or on proxies that stand in for them, are not a gray area
  • Fair housing in listing copy. Describe the property, never the neighbors and never the buyer you imagine. Words about family, safety, walkability to a place of worship, or who a home is perfect for are exactly where this goes wrong
  • Do Not Call, before the list reaches you. Any prospecting list gets scrubbed against federal and state registries and your own internal list, every run. A ranked call list that skipped this step is a fine that has not happened yet
  • Opt out language in automated email. A working unsubscribe, a real physical address, an honest subject line, and a removal that takes effect promptly. This is a legal floor, not a courtesy
  • Nothing reaches a client unread. The approval step is the entire safety system. Volume without a human reading it is how one wrong assumption reaches every person you know at once
  • Client data stays client data. Strip names, addresses and phone numbers out of anything you paste into a general tool. None of this work needs a real identity to function

One more that is not law but should be. Say true things. If the file does not support a claim, it does not go in the message, no matter how well it is written. Compliance and craft point in the same direction here, and both of them come down to reading it before it goes.

Where this stops

This is leverage on the work. It is not a replacement for it.

I would rather you hear the limits from me now than find them yourself in the middle of a transaction. Three of them, and they apply to all seven systems equally.

01

It will not make you calls you do not make

Every system here ends at a conversation you still have to have. What it removes is the friction and the deciding. It does not remove the dialing, and no amount of good tooling ever will.

02

It has no judgment

It does not know that the seller's mother is ill, or that the buyer's tone changed on Thursday. Everything that makes you good at this is still yours to supply, and it is the part clients are actually paying for.

03

It needs maintenance

Markets move, your business changes, and a system built for the way you worked last spring drifts. Budget an hour a month to look at what it is producing and correct it. Nothing here is finished.
